Overflowing toilet: what to do first

When the bowl rises toward the rim, do not flush again. A second flush sends more water into a drain that is already blocked. Cut the supply first.

Most toilets have a small oval valve on the wall or floor behind the bowl. Turn it clockwise until it stops. If it is stuck or leaks when turned, go to the house main, usually where the water line enters the home.

1

Stop the refill

Lift the tank lid and hold the float up so the fill valve quits sending water.

2

Close the supply valve

Turn it clockwise by hand. Do not force a corroded valve.

3

Contain the water

Lay towels at the base and move rugs out of the room.

4

Call before plunging again

If one plunge failed, the blockage may be past the toilet. More plunging only moves water around.

A running toilet is a water bill problem

A toilet that hisses or refills on its own leaks tank water into the bowl and down the drain. It is quiet, so the first sign is often a higher water bill. Three parts cause nearly all of it.

The flapper is a rubber seal that drops to close the tank outlet. When it warps, it stops sealing. The fill valve refills the tank and shuts off as the float rises. If it sticks, water runs over the overflow tube. A float set too high does the same.

To test, put a few drops of food coloring in the tank and wait 15 minutes without flushing. Color in the bowl means a leaking flapper. A water level at the top of the overflow tube points to the fill valve or float.

Leaks at the base and a loose bowl

Dry everything, flush, and watch where the first drop appears. A sweating tank can mimic a leak. If water forms at the floor line, the wax ring has failed. That ring seals the bowl outlet to the floor flange and keeps wastewater and sewer gas in the pipe.

A rocking bowl crushes the ring and starts a slow weep that rots subfloor before it shows. Tighten closet bolts a quarter turn at a time, since hard cranking cracks porcelain.

The fix is to shut off the water, lift the toilet, scrape the old ring, and inspect the flange. A cracked flange gets a repair ring so the bolts have something solid to hold. Then the toilet goes back on a new ring. Slow leaks can also start at supply lines, which our faucet and supply valve repair page covers.

When the plunger will not clear it

A flange plunger with a firm seal clears most clogs in a few strokes. If ten strokes do nothing, something solid sits in the trap. A toilet auger reaches past the bend without scratching porcelain.

If the auger passes and the bowl still backs up, or a tub gurgles when you flush, the blockage is in the line. That is a cable job for our drain cleaning crew. Wipes labeled flushable are a leading cause of repeat clogs. Put them in the trash.

Repair or replace

Repair makes sense when the porcelain is sound and the parts are standard. Replace when the bowl or tank is cracked, the toilet clogs at its own trap, or repairs keep stacking up.

Older tanks often use more water per flush. A tank sized for a bigger flush sends more gallons down the drain on every use, so a lower volume model can cut the water running through your house over a year. Ask us what your tank holds.
