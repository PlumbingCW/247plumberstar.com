Fixing a softener: salt bridges, resin, and control heads

Most softeners fail in a handful of ways. Knowing which one you have decides whether to repair or replace. Here is how the common problems look.

Common softener problems and what they usually mean
What you seeLikely causeRepair or replace?
Salt level never drops, water is hardSalt bridge or mush in the brine tankRepair: break it up and clean the tank
Water hard after regenerationWorn resin, or a fouled resin bedDepends on the age and cost of new resin
Unit regenerates at wrong times or not at allControl head or timer faultRepair if the valve is sound, replace if parts are scarce
Leak at the valve or drainSeals, valve body, or drain lineRepair if parts exist, replace if the body is cracked
Brine tank overflowsFaulty float, valve, or drain restrictionUsually repair

A conditioner that is a different technology, such as a salt-free or template-assisted unit, fails differently. Bring the model name to the call and we will tell you what applies. If an older softener needs repeated repairs, replacement often costs less than the next three visits.

On-demand versus tank-type softeners

Softeners regenerate in one of two ways. A timer type regenerates on a schedule whether or not you used the soft water. An on-demand type measures use and regenerates only when needed, which saves salt and water.

Timer based

Simple, fewer parts. It may regenerate too often or too late if your usage changes.

On-demand (metered)

Tracks gallons used and regenerates when the resin nears capacity. Needs a sound meter and control.

You will also see single-tank and twin-tank designs. Twin tanks keep a second bed ready so soft water does not stop during regeneration. A larger household or a heavy laundry load may justify them.

Salt level never changes or water feels hard?

Call us. A salt bridge is a common and fixable cause, and we can often tell on the phone.

Whole-house filters and under-sink reverse osmosis

A whole-house filter sits on the main line and treats all the water that enters the home. A sediment filter catches grit, and a carbon filter reduces taste and odor. It protects fixtures and appliances and gives every tap treated water, but it does not reduce dissolved minerals the way a softener does.

An under-sink reverse osmosis system treats one faucet, usually the kitchen drinking tap. It pushes water through a fine membrane and sends the rejected water down the drain. It takes up cabinet space and needs a drain connection and filter changes on a schedule.

  • Whole-house filter: one fix for taste, odor, or sediment at every tap.
  • Under-sink reverse osmosis: finer treatment at one faucet, with filter changes.
  • Softener: reduces hardness minerals for scale on fixtures and appliances.
  • Many homes combine a softener with a point-of-use filter, depending on what the test shows.

Bypass valves, drain connections, and pre-plumbed loops

A bypass valve lets you isolate the softener from the house plumbing so you can service it without shutting off water. It is a simple part worth having. The drain line has to run to an approved drain with an air gap, so brine and backwash cannot siphon back.

Idaho's plumbing code adds one rule for new construction. The Idaho State Plumbing Code, IDAPA 24.39.20.600, requires a pre-plumbed water softener loop in new one- and two-family homes built slab on grade or with a finished basement. If you own one, that loop makes a later softener install much simpler. DOPL says a permit is required when plumbing work is performed, so ask who pulls it.

Other installs such as a heater tie-in or new fixtures sit under plumbing installation. A softener upstream of a heater changes how it needs to be piped, and water heater installation covers that. For leaks around the unit, see leak detection. Softener drain connections and bypass valves are plumbed to the 2015 Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) that Idaho adopts as its State Plumbing Code.
