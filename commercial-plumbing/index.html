After-hours leak in a retail or office space: what to do first

Commercial buildings often have more than one shutoff. A suite may have its own valve, the building may have a main in a utility room or mechanical closet, and a tenant may not have access to either. Find the nearest fixture shutoff first, then the suite valve, then the building main.

If water is near outlets, panels, or light fixtures, stay out of the area and call the building owner or property manager for power shutoff. Water and electricity together are the real danger.

In the first 15 minutes

  • ✓Close the closest shutoff and any suite valve you can reach.
  • ✓Call the building owner or property manager if you are a tenant.
  • ✓Move inventory, files, and electronics above the waterline.
  • ✓Photograph the source, the standing water, and damaged items before cleanup.
  • ✓Call us with the address, the space type, and what you see.

Commercial leak detection: what it can and cannot do

Many calls about commercial leaks start with a symptom, such as a wet ceiling tile, a damp wall, a water meter that keeps moving after close, or a bill that jumped. Finding the source is the first step, and we use listening equipment, pressure checks, and cameras where they apply. See our leak detection page for the methods.

Be realistic about scope. A leak inside a wall or slab of a large building can take more than one visit to trace, and a roof or storm drain leak may not be plumbing at all. We tell you what we found and what we did not, rather than selling a repair we cannot confirm.

Commercial drains, floor drains, and lift pumps

A floor drain that gurgles or overflows in a restaurant, shop, or restroom is a warning. It often means the building line is blocked or grease has built up. We clear it with cables, check it with a camera, and use hydro jetting when grease or scale keeps returning. For standard clearing methods, see drain cleaning.

Below-grade restrooms and utility rooms often drain to a lift pump or ejector. When it fails, wastewater can rise in the pit. Turn off nearby fixtures, keep people out of the area, and call. We check the float, the check valve, and the pump motor, and replace the unit when repair makes no sense.

Grease traps and interceptors

A grease trap catches fats and oils before they reach the building drain. When it fills, grease passes through and coats the pipe. The signs are slow kitchen sinks, a sour smell, and repeated drain calls. Cleaning on a regular schedule costs less than a line blocked in the middle of service.

Call and we will tell you whether your unit is one we clean. Large buried interceptors are often pumped by a licensed hauler. Ask your city or sewer provider about requirements for grease disposal and records, since they vary by business.

Backflow testing is planned work, not an emergency

A backflow assembly keeps used water from flowing back into the clean supply. Testing is scheduled, not urgent, and the test must be done by a certified tester. Ask any tester for their current certification before you hire them. We do not claim a certification on this page.

Your water provider or building department can tell you whether your property needs a test and how often. Keep the report, since the provider may ask for it.

Permits, insurance, and documentation

Idaho's Division of Occupational and Professional Licenses says homeowners may buy a plumbing permit only for work on their own residence and related outbuildings, never for commercial buildings. Commercial plumbing work needs a permit pulled by a licensed contractor. Check any plumber's license at DOPL, (208) 334-3233.

For insurance, keep your photos, the date and time, a short list of what was damaged, and every invoice. Tell your insurer and your landlord quickly.
