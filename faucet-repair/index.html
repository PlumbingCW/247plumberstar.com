When a valve will not shut off: emergency steps

Every fixture should have its own shutoff, often called an angle stop or supply stop, under a sink or behind a toilet. Turn it clockwise. If it spins loose, will not move, or leaks around the stem, stop forcing it. An old stop that breaks can make the leak worse.

The next step is the house main. It sits where the water line enters the home, often in a garage, a utility room, a basement, or a crawlspace. It may be a wheel handle or a lever. Turn a wheel clockwise or a lever a quarter turn across the pipe.

Do this in order

  • ✓Close the fixture's own stop under the sink or behind the toilet.
  • ✓If it fails, close the house main and open the lowest faucet to drain the line.
  • ✓Turn off the water heater's power or gas control if the heater may run dry.
  • ✓Move belongings off the floor and photograph the damage for your insurer.
  • ✓If you cannot find or turn the main, call us and stay on the line. We will help you locate it.

Some homes have a second shutoff at the street. Ask the Star Sewer and Water District what you may operate yourself, since ownership varies by address.

Dripping kitchen and bath faucets

A drip is a worn part, not a loose handle. We identify the faucet type and replace the right part rather than tightening a fitting that only masks the leak.

Cartridge faucets

A single cartridge controls flow and temperature. Seals wear and the spout drips. Replacing the cartridge usually ends it.

Ball faucets

A slotted ball with rubber seats and springs sits under the handle. Seats stiffen and spring tension fades.

Ceramic disc faucets

Two polished discs slide against each other. They last, but sediment can scratch them and cause a slow drip.

Compression faucets

Separate hot and cold handles press a rubber washer onto a seat. Worn washers and O-rings drip at the spout or handle.

A faucet that drips from the base or handle is usually an O-ring problem. One that is cracked, pitted, or decades old may cost more to repair than to replace. We will tell you which, and a new fixture often pairs well with a plumbing installation visit.

Broken shutoff valves and failing supply lines

A stop valve can seize for years and then fail the day you need it. Mineral buildup locks the stem, and the packing nut leaks. Replacing a stop is a short job when the pipe behind it is sound, and we check it as we work.

Supply lines are the flexible hoses from the stop to the faucet or toilet. A bulge, a rusted nut, or a kink means it is near failure. A burst line can release a lot of water. If you see wet cabinet floors or swollen wood, close the stop and call. A hidden leak in a wall may need leak detection to trace.

Shower valves, showerheads, and tub spouts

A showerhead that drips after you turn it off usually has a worn cartridge in the valve behind the wall. If the dripping continues and you see a wet stain on a ceiling or wall near the shower, shut the water off to that fixture and call. A valve body that leaks inside a wall can damage drywall quietly.

A tub spout that keeps running has a worn cartridge or diverter. Replacing a cartridge is usually a small job, while a worn valve body may mean opening the wall behind the tub.

Slow tub drains and outdoor hose bibs

A tub or shower that drains slowly is usually a hair and soap plug close to the fixture. If more than one drain is sluggish, the problem is further down the line, and our drain cleaning crew clears it with a cable. For a sink that gurgles and backs up, start there as well.

Hose bibs, the outdoor spigots, can split inside the wall if water froze in them. The damage often shows only in spring, when you turn the water on and it sprays inside the house. Disconnect hoses before cold weather, and call if a bib leaks from behind the handle or sprays through the wall.

A like-for-like faucet swap is simple, but moving or replacing supply lines is plumbing work under the 2015 Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), which Idaho adopts as its State Plumbing Code.
