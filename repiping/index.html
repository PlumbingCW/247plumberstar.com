Signs your house is ready for new pipes

One leak is a repair. A pattern is a message. When pipes fail in several places, each patch only moves the next failure down the line.

Repeated pinhole leaks

Small leaks in different spots usually mean the pipe wall is thin along its whole length.

Rusty or brown water

Galvanized steel rusts from the inside. Water that runs brown, especially first thing, is a warning.

Low pressure through the house

Corrosion narrows the pipe bore, so pressure falls even when the supply is fine.

Visible corrosion

Green crust on copper, flaking, or white buildup at fittings means the metal is wearing away.

Discolored water can have other sources, including the water heater or work on a main, so a plumber should look before you decide. Our leak detection service can confirm whether a hidden leak is part of the picture.

Galvanized pipe replacement

Galvanized steel pipe is steel coated in zinc. Over decades the coating wears off and rust forms inside, which shrinks the opening and sheds flakes. It cannot be cleaned out, so replacement is the real fix. We can tell you whether a house has galvanized pipe by looking at it and testing it, rather than guessing by neighborhood or year.

PEX, copper, or CPVC: honest trade-offs

Common repipe materials compared
MaterialStrengthsTrade-offs
PEXFlexible, so it snakes through walls with fewer fittings and less opened drywallNot for outdoor sun exposure. Certain fittings and tools matter for a good connection
CopperLong track record, rigid, handles heat well, and can be repairedMore labor to install, more joints, and some water chemistry can eat at it over time
CPVCRigid plastic that works for hot and cold supply and resists corrosionCan turn brittle with age and heat, and fittings need careful solvent work

No material is right for every house. We consider your wall construction, attic or crawlspace access, water chemistry, and budget, and explain why we recommend what we do. If another plumber insists on one material for every house, ask why.

What a repipe involves

1

Walkthrough and plan

We trace the existing lines, choose access points, and decide where new pipe will run.

2

Water off, access opened

We shut the water, then cut small openings in drywall, usually in closets, behind fixtures, and in attic or crawlspace areas.

3

New lines installed

New supply pipe is run to each fixture, connected at the main and the water heater, and the old lines are abandoned or removed.

4

Test and inspect

The system is pressure tested, flushed, and inspected before walls are closed.

We do not promise a duration, because it depends on the house, the number of fixtures, and what we find. We tell you the plan in advance, and you will not be without water longer than needed.

Drywall access and patching: what to expect

Repiping does open walls. PEX needs fewer and smaller holes than rigid pipe, but there will be some. We cut access panels where they are easiest to repair, and we patch them to a paint-ready state. Painting and texture matching are usually separate work, and we tell you up front what is included so there are no surprises.

Permits and inspections in Idaho

Idaho requires a permit when plumbing work is performed. Required inspections are ground work, rough-in, and final. Homeowners may buy a permit only for plumbing work on their own residence, never for commercial buildings, and unlicensed plumbing carries civil penalties.

Inspections are requested by phone at 208-334-3950 or online. A local city building permit may not replace the state permit, so ask your city building department about its own rules. You can verify any plumber through the state licensing division at (208) 334-3233.
